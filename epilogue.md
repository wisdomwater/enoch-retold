## Epilogue

We have traveled a long way with Enoch.

We have crossed the boundaries of the familiar world and wandered through a creation stranger than the one we ordinarily imagine. We have stood beneath mountains whose peaks seemed to touch heaven, watched stars move through their appointed gates, felt winds released from hidden chambers, and looked into abysses burning with fire. We have encountered angels beautiful and terrifying, creatures belonging to forgotten ages, heavenly tablets upon which human deeds are remembered, and mysteries that resist every attempt to explain them.

We have also seen darkness.

We have watched the Watchers abandon their appointed place and seen violence spread through the earth. We have witnessed giants devour, rulers oppress, shepherds betray the sheep entrusted to them, and generations mistake power for righteousness. Again and again, evil has appeared enormous—so deeply rooted in the world that goodness seemed almost impossibly small beside it.

And yet the darkness never received the final word.

Again and again, light returned. The prayers of the oppressed rose into heaven. The forgotten were remembered. The proud discovered that their towers could fall, their gold could vanish, and their power could not purchase permanence. The sword was finally returned to the house of God and sealed away, the scattered were gathered, and a house greater than the former one rose upon the earth.

Perhaps that is what I hope remains with you when you close this book.

Not the measurements of the heavens or the names of angels. Not the strange creatures or the chambers of the stars, fascinating though they may be. Not even the mysteries of Enoch's visions, many of which have puzzled readers for thousands of years.

I hope you remember the light.

There will always be voices telling us that goodness is too weak for the world we inhabit. When confronted with hatred, hatred seems like the stronger weapon. When confronted with oppression, domination can appear to be the only language an oppressor understands. When falsehood shouts, we may become convinced that truth must learn to shout louder.

But there is a danger hidden there.

If we fight hatred by learning to hate more fiercely, hatred has already gained something from us. If we defeat an oppressor only by becoming oppressive ourselves, the throne may have changed occupants while the kingdom remains unchanged. Darkness does not care whose hands carry it.

The difficult calling is to resist evil without allowing evil to teach us who we must become.

This does not mean passivity. Enoch's visions certainly do not ask us to pretend that injustice is harmless. He cries out against exploitation, falsehood, violence, and those who build their comfort upon the suffering of others. There are things worth resisting, truths worth defending, wounds worth tending, and people worth protecting.

But we can resist without surrendering our souls to hatred.

We can speak truth without delighting in humiliation. We can confront injustice without losing compassion. We can protect the vulnerable without needing to dehumanize those who have forgotten their own humanity. We can refuse to cooperate with darkness while remembering that our task is not merely to defeat darkness around us, but to keep it from taking root within us.

And perhaps we can learn to pursue goodness without always asking what it will earn us.

Do good because goodness is beautiful.

Tell the truth because truth is worth telling. Show mercy because mercy is worth bringing into the world. Feed someone because they are hungry. Forgive because you refuse to allow an old wound to dictate the future. Defend someone who is being mistreated because their dignity matters, even if no one notices what you have done.

Much of this will never be celebrated.

There may be no monument to the moment when you chose patience instead of cruelty. No one may remember the argument in which you could have wounded someone and chose not to. History may never record the small kindness that interrupted another person's despair.

But Enoch has shown us a heaven that remembers.

So now we return from his strange world to our own.

The mountains around us may not blaze with heavenly fire. We may never see the gates through which the stars pass or hear an angel explain the hidden chambers of creation. Tomorrow will probably greet us with ordinary things—work to be done, people to love, frustrations to endure, decisions to make, and opportunities so small that they hardly seem capable of changing anything.

Perhaps that is precisely where the path begins.

Enoch was, after all, remembered first for something wonderfully simple.

He walked with God.

So walk.

Walk gently through a world that often rewards force. Walk truthfully through a world comfortable with illusion. Walk mercifully through a world eager to divide people into those worthy and unworthy of compassion.

When darkness becomes bold, do not let its boldness deceive you into believing it is eternal.

When hatred becomes loud, do not mistake its volume for strength.

When oppression appears immovable, remember the mountains we watched tremble.

And when you cannot change the whole world, tend faithfully to the small portion of it placed within your reach.

Carry the light there.

Then keep walking.
