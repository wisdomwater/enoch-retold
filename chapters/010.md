## 10 - Where the Dead Await

I stood trembling before the abyss while columns of fire plunged into depths my eyes could not measure. Then Uriel spoke to me. “Enoch, why are you alarmed and amazed by this terrible place and by the suffering you see?” I could scarcely imagine why anyone would *not* tremble, but he told me plainly: this was the prison prepared for the angels who had rebelled, and there they would remain.

We departed from that dreadful place, and I was grateful to turn my eyes away from it. Yet what came next carried a different solemnity. Toward the west I saw a great mountain rising before us, a mass of strong rock containing within itself four immense places. They were strangely beautiful, yet deep and dark, their surfaces smooth as though some enormous hand had polished the stone.

Raphael was beside me then, and he told me what I was seeing.

“These places were prepared for the spirits of the dead.”

I looked again, and the mountain no longer seemed empty. Within those vast chambers were gathered the souls of the sons and daughters of men, generation upon generation hidden from the eyes of the living. They would remain there until the appointed time, Raphael told me, waiting through the long years until the great judgment.

Then I heard voices.

You who still dwell among the living know death chiefly by its silence. A mouth speaks to you one day and is still the next. A chair remains empty; footsteps no longer cross the threshold; a name that once belonged to a breathing person becomes something spoken in memory. But in that mountain I learned that the silence of the dead is not the same as their disappearance.

One voice rose above the others.

It reached toward heaven with such persistence that I asked Raphael, “Whose spirit is this that cries out and accuses?”

“This is Abel,” he answered, “whom Cain his brother killed.”

At the sound of that name, the earliest grief of humanity seemed suddenly near. I thought of two brothers walking upon the young earth, of jealousy ripening in secret, of one hand rising against another, and of blood entering soil that had never before received the blood of a murdered man. Long ages had passed, yet Abel's voice had not vanished.

The wound still spoke.

I remembered how the earth itself had cried beneath the violence of the giants. Now I heard the same mystery within a single human soul. What is done in secret does not become unreal because the world moves onward. The forgotten victim is not forgotten simply because the murderer has returned to dust.

I asked Raphael why the spirits were separated from one another.

He showed me divisions within those hidden places. The spirits of the righteous had been set apart, with a chasm between them and the others, with water and light above. Elsewhere were those who had practiced wickedness and had passed from the earth without seeing justice during their mortal lives. Death had not made righteousness and cruelty indistinguishable.

This troubled me, but it also answered something I had carried in my heart.

I had watched violent men prosper. I had seen those who oppressed others grow old while their victims went into the earth. Every generation knows this bitterness: sometimes the cruel die peacefully, surrounded by wealth, while the gentle disappear without vindication. If death were merely a curtain falling upon all things, then many stories would end with injustice holding the final word.

But the chambers beneath the mountain told me otherwise.

The dead waited.

The wounded were remembered.

The deeds of a human life were not erased simply because the body returned to the ground.

Yet I did not leave that place eager for vengeance. Hearing Abel's voice did not awaken in me a desire to become another Cain. This is one of the great temptations of the wounded heart: when blood cries out, we may imagine that healing requires more blood. But if every wound creates another wound, the earth becomes an endless field of brothers killing brothers.

Justice must belong finally to One whose sight is clearer than ours.

So I blessed the Lord of glory and righteousness, the One who sees both the hand that strikes and the heart that bleeds. I entrusted to him what I could not resolve. There is peace in refusing to become the judge of every soul, though there is also courage in refusing to call evil good.

From that mountain we traveled westward toward the extremities of the earth.

There I saw a fire running without ceasing. Day did not diminish it, and night did not increase it. It streamed onward with the same unwavering brightness, and I asked Raguel, who traveled with me, what fire could burn with such constancy.

He told me that it belonged to the luminaries of heaven.

Again we journeyed, until a mountain of fire appeared before me, flashing day and night. Beyond it rose seven splendid mountains, each different from the others, their stones brilliant with colors and beauty unlike the common rock beneath human feet. After the darkness of the chambers of the dead, their radiance seemed almost unbearable.

And as I approached them, I carried Abel's voice with me.

I had traveled from the prison of angels to the dwelling of the dead, from darkness beneath a mountain to fire running at the edge of the world. Everywhere I went, I discovered that creation remembers.

Perhaps this is why you need not answer every darkness with darkness.

Your wounds are not made invisible because you refuse revenge. Your gentleness does not erase the truth of what was done to you. The cry of Abel crossed ages and reached heaven without Abel rising from the earth to strike his brother in return.

I heard his voice.

More importantly, heaven heard it.

And beneath all the fires, judgments, hidden chambers, and mysteries I could scarcely comprehend, I began to trust this:

Nothing given to God is forgotten.

Not even the voice of the one the world has silenced.
