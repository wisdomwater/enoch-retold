## 34 - When the Earth Fell Away

There were still names and measures I entrusted to you, Mathusala—the conductors Gedaeyal, Keel, and Heel, and Asphael, leader of a thousand, whose appointed days came to their completion. I wrote them as they were given to me, for I had learned that heaven forgets neither the great nor the small. Every light has its station, every season its boundary, and every servant its appointed hour.

But there was another vision I had not yet told you. I saw it long before your birth, before I had taken your mother as my wife, when I was still young and learning from books rather than writing them. I had not yet walked the strange roads I have described to you, nor stood beside angels at the extremities of creation.

I was lying in the house of my grandfather Malalel when the vision seized me.

The heaven above me began to change. It seemed to be swept clean and drawn away, as though an unseen hand had taken hold of the great covering stretched over the world and pulled it from its place. The familiar sky vanished, and suddenly there was nothing above the earth to shelter it.

Then the earth itself began to fail.

I saw the ground open into a vast abyss, deeper than any valley and darker than any night. Mountains hung above mountains with nothing beneath them, immense masses of stone suspended over emptiness, while hills collapsed upon other hills and disappeared. Trees that had stood for generations were torn from their trunks and swept away, their roots exposed for a moment before they plunged into the darkness below.

The sound was terrible. Stone groaned against stone, forests cracked like dry reeds, and the earth seemed to cry out beneath its own undoing. Everything I had thought immovable was moving, and everything I had trusted as solid was being swallowed.

My courage left me.

“The earth is destroyed!” I cried.

But even those words barely escaped me. My voice faltered, and it seemed as though the speech itself had fallen from my mouth. I was only a young man before a vision too large for me, watching the world disappear into an abyss I could neither measure nor understand.

Then I felt my grandfather's hands upon me.

Malalel raised me and asked why I was crying and what grief had overtaken me. I told him everything: the heavens drawn away, the mountains suspended, the hills collapsing, the trees torn loose, and the earth sinking into darkness. I expected him to soothe me by saying that it had been only a dream.

He did not.

“What you have seen is established, my son,” he said.

His face grew heavy as he told me that the vision concerned the hidden corruption of the earth. Secret wickedness does not remain secret forever. Violence buried beneath custom, greed concealed beneath abundance, cruelty hidden behind strength—all of it enters the fabric of the world until what men have built can no longer bear the weight of what they have become.

A great destruction would come.

Yet my grandfather did not tell me to rejoice.

He did not say, “The wicked will perish; therefore be glad.” Instead he told me to rise and pray. If I was faithful, then faithfulness in the presence of judgment meant intercession.

“Beseech the Lord of glory,” he told me. “Ask that a remnant may remain upon the earth.”

Those words entered me more deeply than the terror of the vision.

I had seen a world corrupted by hidden sin, yet I was taught not to hate it. I had seen destruction approaching, yet I was told to ask for mercy. It is easy for a man who believes himself righteous to look upon the wicked and desire their ruin; there is a strange sweetness pride finds in imagining that judgment will fall upon another house.

But my grandfather taught me a better way.

So I rose and prayed.

I pleaded with the Lord of glory for the earth I had just seen collapsing. I prayed for generations not yet born, for children whose names I did not know, for men and women who would walk beneath skies I would never see. I asked that destruction would not have the final word, that something living might remain when the waters of judgment had passed.

And I wrote my prayer down.

Later I went outside.

After the vision, ordinary morning seemed like a miracle. I lifted my eyes and saw the sun emerging from the east exactly as it had been commanded. In the west the moon was descending, and scattered stars still lingered in the paling darkness.

Nothing appeared to have changed.

The mountains stood where they had stood the day before. Trees remained rooted in the earth. The ground beneath my feet was firm, and the heavens stretched peacefully above me.

Yet I could no longer look upon these things carelessly.

I blessed the Lord of judgment because the sun still came forth from its eastern chamber and traveled the path prepared for it. I blessed him because after seeing the world fall apart, I had awakened to find light once again moving across the face of heaven.

Since that day, I have known that judgment and mercy are not as easily separated as men suppose. Sometimes judgment is the terrible ending of what has been destroying the earth. And sometimes mercy appears as a grandfather telling a frightened young man not to curse the darkness he has seen, but to pray for those who must pass through it.

Remember this when you behold evil, Mathusala.

Do not become what you condemn.

If you see the earth wounded, pray for the earth. If you see men wandering toward destruction, do not feed their darkness with hatred of your own. Stand before the Lord and ask that something may be saved, something healed, something carried through.

For long before I learned to read the courses of heaven, my grandfather taught me the first work of a seer:

When you are shown destruction, pray for life.
