## 7 - You Were Made for Heaven

My eyes remained fixed upon the ground. Fire moved before the throne, myriads stood beyond my sight, and I could still feel the trembling of the crystal chambers through my body. Then the voice came to me from the glory, not like the voice of thunder that drives a man into hiding, but clear enough that every word entered me. “Hear, Enoch. Do not be afraid. Approach and listen.”

So I lifted myself from the place where I had fallen, though fear had not entirely left me. Perhaps there is a fear that belongs to darkness and another that belongs to wonder. The first makes the heart flee because it expects harm; the second makes the heart bow because it has encountered something too immense to possess. Before the Great One, I knew that I was safe enough to listen and small enough not to presume.

Then he spoke of the Watchers who had sent me.

“Go and say to them: You should have prayed for humanity, not humanity for you.”

The words pierced me. These beings had once stood in the heights, watching over creatures weaker than themselves. Their strength had been given as stewardship, their knowledge as service, their heavenly station as a calling. Yet now they sat with veiled faces waiting for a mortal scribe to carry their petitions upward.

The Holy One asked them why they had abandoned the lofty heaven. They had been made spiritual, belonging to a realm that endured beyond the brief generations of flesh, yet they had looked upon the daughters of men and desired to possess what belonged to another order. Human beings marry and bring forth children because our lives pass quickly. We are born like grass beneath the morning sun, we flourish for a season, and eventually we return to the ground from which we came.

So children are given to humanity. One generation receives life and passes it onward to another. A mother holds an infant who may someday stand beside her grave; a father teaches a son whose eyes may see mornings he himself will never behold. There is sadness in this, but also tenderness, for mortality teaches us to give away what we cannot keep.

The Watchers were different.

Their dwelling had been heaven. Their life belonged to the spiritual order, and what had been given to mortals because of mortality had not been appointed to them. Yet they descended, grasped what they desired, and attempted to mingle what had not been given to them to mingle. From that crossing came the giants—creatures born between spirit and flesh, belonging wholly to neither.

Then I was shown what would become of them.

Their enormous bodies would perish, but something of them would remain upon the earth. From the flesh of the giants would proceed spirits restless and disordered, carrying within themselves the confusion of their origin. Because their beginning had come partly from those who descended from heaven, yet their bodies had been born upon earth, they would remain bound to the earthly realm.

I saw them moving like clouds.

Not the gentle clouds that gather rain and shade the fields, but dark masses driven low over the earth, pressing upon those beneath them. They would oppress and corrupt, contend and bruise. They would move unseen through places of sorrow, bringing lamentation and disorder, restless because nothing of the material world could satisfy them.

They would hunger without eating.

They would thirst without drinking.

As I listened, I remembered the giants themselves. Nothing had satisfied them either. They had consumed the crops of men, then animals, then human beings, yet their appetite only widened. Now even without bodies their hunger would remain—a terrible image of desire separated from any possibility of fulfillment.

This taught me to fear not desire itself, but desire that has forgotten its proper home.

Every creature has a place within the order of God. The stars have their courses, the rivers their channels, the trees their seasons, humanity its brief and beautiful generations, and the spirits of heaven their dwelling above. Peace exists when power receives its boundaries as gift rather than insult. Disorder begins when the heart says, *What has been given to me is not enough; I must also possess what has been given to another.*

The Watchers had looked downward and grasped.

Their children had devoured.

Their children's spirits would wander hungry.

I began to see a single thread running through it all.

Grasping does not satisfy hunger. It creates a greater hunger.

Perhaps you know something of this even without seeing giants or wandering spirits. There are appetites within the human heart that promise rest if only they are obeyed one more time. Possess this. Conquer that. Be admired. Be feared. Take what you desire. Answer the wound with another wound.

But I have watched where that road leads.

So when darkness whispers that peace will come when you finally possess enough, remember the restless spirits of the giants. Remember the Watchers who abandoned heaven because something beyond their appointed life appeared desirable. Remember beings who possessed wonders beyond human imagining and still convinced themselves that they lacked something necessary.

Then consider the tree.

It does not envy the river.

The river does not demand the path of the star.

And the star does not descend from its course to become the mountain.

Each receives its place within the great mystery, and in receiving rather than grasping, creation remains whole.

I had traveled through fire to hear the voice of the Eternal, and there before the throne I began to understand: sometimes holiness is not reaching higher or taking more.

Sometimes holiness is learning to inhabit, with gratitude, the life you have been given.
