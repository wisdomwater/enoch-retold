## 5 - The Scribe Between Heaven and Earth

I heard the voice of the Eternal speak over the wounded earth: “Purify it.” The command reached farther than the punishment of the Watchers or the ending of the giants, for the sickness had entered deeply into the world. Oppression, injustice, violence, impiety—all that had polluted the soil and filled creation with suffering was to be uprooted. Then I was shown an earth where peace and equity walked among humanity as companions, not for a fleeting season, but from generation to generation.

The treasures of heaven were opened above it. Blessing descended like rain upon the labor of human hands, and nations once divided by violence turned together toward the One who had made them. The earth was cleansed not only from crime, but from suffering itself, and I heard the promise that the great waters would never again be sent to consume it. After all I had seen of blood and corruption, it seemed almost impossible that such peace could exist.

Yet before these things came to pass, my own path entered the judgment I had witnessed.

In those days I was concealed from the sons of men. Few knew where I had gone or what had happened to me, for my company had become the holy ones, and my days were filled with things difficult to carry back into ordinary speech. I blessed the Great Lord, the King of peace, and among the heavenly beings I became known by a name that still humbles me: Enoch the scribe.

A scribe does not command the word entrusted to him. He receives it.

“Enoch, scribe of righteousness,” the Lord said to me, “go to the Watchers of heaven.”

I knew immediately of whom he spoke. They had abandoned the lofty places, leaving the station appointed to them in the everlasting heights. They had descended into desire, taken wives from among the daughters of men, revealed things that should have remained hidden, and filled the earth with consequences they had not foreseen. Now I was commanded to carry a word back to beings before whom ordinary men would have trembled.

They would have no peace.

I went first to Azazyel. Perhaps you imagine that because I carried the judgment of heaven I felt powerful as I approached him, but I did not. There is little triumph in standing before a condemned creature when you have seen enough of heaven to know how small every creature is. I told him only what had been given to me: a sentence had gone forth against him because he had taught oppression, blasphemy, tyranny, and sin, and he would be bound.

Then I went among the others.

The Watchers trembled.

I had seen these beings descend from heaven and alter the course of the earth, yet now they stood afraid before a man made from dust. Their terror was not of me. Something far more terrible had happened: they could no longer lift their eyes toward heaven.

They gathered around me and begged me to write.

“Make a memorial for us,” they pleaded. “Carry our supplication before the God of heaven. Ask that we might be forgiven.”

Their faces were heavy with shame, and I found that I could not look upon their sorrow without feeling sorrow myself. This is a mystery I have carried ever since: one may speak truth about evil without learning to delight in the suffering of the one who committed it. Judgment had been spoken, yet I was still willing to carry their prayer.

So I wrote.

I wrote of their spirits and their deeds, of their fear and their longing for remission. I gathered the cries of those who had once inhabited heaven into human words and carried the memorial with me toward the waters of Danbadan, west of Armon. There I read their petition beneath the open sky.

I wonder whether they watched me from afar.

The waters moved beside me while I read, and Mount Armon stood in the distance, still bearing the memory of their oath. Once they had stood upon that mountain binding themselves together in rebellion; now they waited for one frail human being to carry their words back toward the heaven they could no longer face. Such is the strange reversal of pride. Those who had reached downward to seize what was not theirs could no longer raise their eyes toward what they had abandoned.

As I continued reading, sleep overtook me.

But my sleep did not bring rest.

A vision opened above me, and I fell down beneath its weight. I saw punishment and things appointed for the sons of heaven, things I knew I would have to speak aloud when I returned to them. The darkness of the vision pressed upon me until even in sleep I understood that their memorial had not erased what had been done.

When I awoke, I went to find them.

They were gathered at Oubelseyael, between Libanos and Seneser. The beings who had once looked down from heaven upon the daughters of men now sat together weeping. Their faces were veiled, as though even the light upon the earth had become difficult for them to endure.

I stood before them carrying their prayer and the vision I had received.

There I learned something about walking in the light that I have never forgotten. Light does not require us to hate those who have entered darkness. Neither does mercy require us to call darkness light.

I could write their prayer.

I could stand among their tears.

I could grieve for what they had become.

And still I had to tell them what I had seen.
