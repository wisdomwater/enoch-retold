## 12 - Where Heaven Opens

I left the desert behind me and journeyed eastward from the mountain I had approached. The land changed as I went, and before long I came among trees unlike those I had known in the habitations of men. Their branches were heavy with fragrant resin, and the air itself seemed clothed in sweetness, for there grew the trees of frankincense and myrrh, each giving its own perfume to the wind.

Beyond them rose the eastern mountain, not far away, standing like a dark wall above a garden of incense. I walked beneath its height while the scents gathered around me, and I wondered at the hidden abundance of the earth. There are places in creation that men never see, yet they flower without witnesses and pour out their fragrance before God as though His delight alone were enough.

I passed onward and came to valleys filled with water that never failed. No drought diminished them, no scorching wind emptied their channels, and along their banks grew a beautiful tree whose fragrance reached me before I stood beneath its branches. Cinnamon grew there also, breathing sweetness from the sides of the valley, so that every step carried me deeper into a land where even the air seemed alive.

From there I came to another mountain, and water flowed from among its trees in clear streams. I learned the names of those places and their growths—Sarira, Kalboneba, and beyond them trees called Alva, strong and full like almond trees heavy with life. When their fruit ripened, its fragrance surpassed every perfume I had known, and I began to understand that the world visible to men is only a small courtyard before a house of immeasurable rooms.

Yet I was not shown these wonders so that I might possess them. I could not gather that fragrance into my hands or carry those valleys home with me. I could only receive what was given to my sight and continue onward, and this too was a lesson: much of what is holy must be beheld without being grasped.

Toward the north I saw seven mountains clothed with pure nard, cinnamon, papyrus, and trees whose perfumes mingled upon their slopes. I passed above their summits and traveled far eastward, crossing beyond the Erythraean Sea until even the lands familiar to men had vanished behind me. There, beyond the angel Zateel, I came at last to the garden of righteousness.

Reader, I cannot easily tell you the beauty of that place. The trees were great and flourishing, varied in form, their branches spreading beneath a purity of light unlike the light that falls upon our fields. Their fragrance was powerful but not oppressive, and as I walked among them I felt that I had entered a memory older than mankind.

Among those trees stood one that drew my eyes above all the others.

Its form resembled a tamarind, yet its fruit hung like clusters of delicate grapes, radiant and inviting. Its fragrance traveled far through the garden, and there was something about it that stirred both wonder and unease within me, as though beauty and sorrow had once met beneath its branches. I cried aloud, “How beautiful is this tree, and how delightful its appearance!”

Raphael, the holy angel who walked beside me, answered me. “This is the tree of knowledge. Your ancient father and your aged mother ate from it; their eyes were opened, they knew their nakedness, and they were driven from the garden.”

I stood before it in silence.

The tree itself was not twisted or ugly. Its fruit did not bear the appearance of death, and there was no warning written upon its bark. That troubled me more deeply than if I had found it covered in thorns, for I saw that not everything that wounds us first arrives wearing the face of darkness.

There are desires that appear beautiful because they promise sight. There are things the heart reaches toward because it wishes to know, to possess, to become greater than it is. Yet there is a wisdom that comes from receiving and another kind that is seized before its hour, and I have learned that the difference between them may be hidden until the fruit has already been eaten.

I left the garden and traveled toward the ends of the earth. There I saw enormous beasts unlike one another and birds of many shapes and faces, each crying out in its own strange voice. Creation became wilder the farther I traveled, as though the familiar patterns of the world were falling away and I had reached regions where God had never been constrained by the expectations of men.

Then I came to the place where the earth itself seemed to end.

Heaven opened before me.

Gates stood in the heights, and through them I saw the stars come forth one by one. They did not wander without order, as men sometimes imagine when they look upward into the night; each emerged according to its appointed number, time, and season. Uriel stood beside me and showed me their names, their paths, their regulations, and their works, and I wrote what he revealed as carefully as I could.

I had once looked upon the stars as distant fires scattered across darkness. Now I saw them passing through gates like servants answering a summons. Their movements were vast beyond my understanding, yet nothing in their courses seemed forgotten.

This gave me courage.

For if God remembers the path of a star, how quickly we despair when we cannot see the path of our own small lives.

From there I journeyed north and saw three heavenly gates. Through them came the winds bearing cold, hail, frost, snow, dew, and rain; through one gate the wind moved gently, but through the others it rushed with terrible strength across the earth. I later saw corresponding gates in the west, the south, and again in the east, where smaller gates opened within the greater ones and the stars passed through on their journeys toward the west.

I began to see creation not as a silent emptiness but as a vast ordering of doors, seasons, winds, waters, lights, and appointed paths. Some forces descended softly like dew upon grass; others came violently enough to shake forests and scour the mountains. Yet both gentle wind and storm emerged from gates I had been permitted to behold.

And so I tell you this: do not mistake peace for the absence of power.

The One who sends the dew also knows the path of the storm. The One who tends the hidden trees of incense also governs the stars moving above nations that believe themselves mighty. Our task is not to command these gates, nor to grasp every mystery hidden behind them, but to walk faithfully beneath their vastness.

I had seen the tree through which humanity reached for knowledge, and I had seen the heavens where stars obeyed the paths appointed to them. Between those two visions I began to understand something of the human heart. We suffer when we try to become masters of mysteries that were meant first to teach us wonder.

Walk gently, then, beneath the opened heavens.

There is more order above you than you can see, more beauty beyond you than you can possess, and more mystery within creation than your hands were ever meant to hold.
