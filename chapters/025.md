## 25 - When Michael Wept

The things shown to Noah were gathered among the words entrusted to me, signs of mysteries that had been hidden from the generations of men. Some secrets were written plainly, others concealed within parables, like stars glimpsed through clouds, their light visible though their distance could not be measured. I learned not to despise what I could not understand, for the heavens contain truths too large to be carried whole within the mind of a man.

Then I heard Michael speaking with Raphael.

Until that hour I had known Michael as one of the great holy ones, terrible in strength, standing before powers that would have caused armies to flee. Yet when he spoke of the judgment prepared for the rebellious angels, his voice was troubled. “The power of the spirit carries me away,” he said, “for who can behold the severity of this secret judgment and not melt at the sight of it?”

I listened in astonishment. Heaven did not gaze upon judgment with cold eyes. Michael did not rejoice because his enemies suffered, nor did he speak as a warrior delighted by victory; the sight wounded him, and he asked Raphael who could witness such a thing without the heart becoming soft and the inward parts trembling.

This taught me something I had not expected to learn among angels.

There is a hardness that men sometimes mistake for righteousness. We imagine that if a judgment is deserved, compassion must become silent, as though pity were a betrayal of truth. But Michael knew the rebellion of these angels more clearly than I did, and still his heart was moved by what had come upon them.

Their judgment had gone forth because they had stood before the Lord of Spirits and rejected the place given to them. They had descended, corrupted, taught forbidden things, and used heavenly power as though it belonged to them. Rakael spoke concerning them and said they had conducted themselves like lords, taking for themselves a dominion that was never theirs to possess.

This was the root beneath many of the horrors I had seen.

They wished to be as masters.

Power is a strange burden. When it is received with humility, it can shelter the weak, establish peace, and cause life to flourish beneath it. But when the hand closes around power and says, *This is mine*, the gift becomes a throne, and the one who sits upon it soon begins to imagine that other lives exist for his pleasure.

So it had been with the Watchers.

Their judgment belonged to them alone. Neither angel nor man would bear their portion for them, for each would receive the consequence of the path he had chosen. Yet even as this sentence was spoken, I remembered Michael's troubled voice, and I understood that justice in heaven does not require the extinguishing of compassion.

Afterward I was shown again the names of those who had led the rebellion. Samyaza, Arstikapha, Armen, Kakabael, Turel, Rumyel, Danyal, Kael, Barakel, Azazel, Armers, Bataryal, Basasael, Ananel, Turyal, Simapiseel, Yetarel, Tumael, Tarel, Rumel, Azazyel. Their names were preserved because evil does not become less real when men refuse to name it.

They had ranks among themselves—leaders of hundreds, leaders of fifties, leaders of tens. Even rebellion had fashioned its own order. I found this terrible in its own way, for darkness does not always arrive as chaos; sometimes it marches in ranks, gives titles to its servants, and calls obedience to violence a virtue.

Then I heard of Yekun, who drew the sons of the holy angels away and led them downward toward the earth. Kesabel gave them corrupt counsel, persuading them to mingle themselves with humanity. Step followed step, desire followed suggestion, and what perhaps began as a turning of the gaze became a descent from which whole generations would suffer.

Gadrel was named among them, and his teaching chilled me. He revealed to humanity the strokes of death. He taught the making of armor and shields, and placed the sword into human imagination—not merely the sharpened metal, but the knowledge of how efficiently one man might destroy another.

I saw in my mind the generations that would follow: furnaces burning, hammers falling, blades lifted beneath the sun. A thing drawn from the earth would be shaped by human hands and returned to the body of another man as death. Knowledge had increased, yet wisdom had not increased beside it.

The angel also spoke of Eve and of the ancient deception, and I felt again how old this wound truly was. The temptation was always similar: reach beyond what has been given, seize what appears desirable, become greater by possessing what was never yours to command. Angels had fallen by it, kings had built empires upon it, and ordinary hearts carried its seed in quieter forms.

Yet I could not forget Michael.

He stood before all this wickedness without pretending it was harmless. He named rebellion as rebellion and accepted that judgment must come. But he did not allow the darkness of those he judged to create darkness within himself.

His heart remained soft.

Perhaps that is among the hardest forms of holiness: to look directly upon evil without calling it good, to resist it without becoming its mirror, to desire its ending without delighting in the ruin of those caught within it.

I had seen angels of fire and mountains of metal. I had watched the deep tremble and heard judgments that shook creation.

But among the mysteries shown to me, I count this also as a wonder:

Michael wept.
