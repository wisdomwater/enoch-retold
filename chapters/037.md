## 37 - Through the Waters and the Wilderness

The sheep multiplied in that foreign pasture, but the wolves did not look upon their abundance with peace. They began to frighten them, oppress them, and destroy their young, casting the little ones into torrents of deep water. I heard the cries of mothers over the waters, and the whole flock seemed to bend beneath a grief too heavy for creatures so defenseless to carry.

Then the sheep cried to their Lord.

They did not possess the strength of the wolves, and no weapon appeared among them by which they could overcome their masters. Their suffering itself became their prayer. They moaned and called upward with all their might until the Lord of the sheep heard them from his lofty habitation.

And he descended.

I saw him come to inspect the flock, and his presence changed the vision. He called to the sheep who had escaped from the wolves and gone away among the wild asses, and he sent him back carrying a word: the wolves were to release the sheep. Another joined him, and together they entered the dwelling of those who had oppressed their people.

But the wolves would not listen.

They increased their violence, and again the sheep cried out. Then the Lord of the sheep moved against the wolves, and the oppressors who had caused so much lamentation began themselves to lament. The sheep became silent.

I watched the flock depart.

The wolves, blinded by their own fury, pursued them, while the Lord himself went with his sheep. His appearance was terrible and glorious, bright with a majesty before which I could scarcely look. Yet every sheep followed him.

Their path brought them to a great lake.

Behind them came the wolves. Before them lay the water. I could see no road by which the sheep might escape, but suddenly the lake divided, its waters rising on either side, and the Lord placed himself between the flock and those pursuing them.

The sheep crossed.

The wolves rushed after them, unable even then to release what they believed belonged to them. They entered the path through the waters, but there they perceived the Lord of the sheep and turned in terror. Before they could escape, the lake returned to its nature.

The waters fell.

Every wolf that had pursued the flock disappeared beneath them.

Again I felt no joy in watching creatures drown. Yet I understood something I had learned before: there are moments when judgment becomes the breaking of a chain. The sheep could not be free while the wolves retained the power to drag them back into bondage.

But freedom did not lead immediately into a garden.

The sheep entered a wilderness without water or grass. There the Lord gave them what they needed, and something stranger began to happen: their eyes opened. Creatures that had lived beneath oppression were learning to see.

The sheep who had led them from the wolves climbed a lofty rock, and the Lord spoke with him there. The rest remained below, trembling before the terrible splendor of their Lord. “We cannot stand before him,” they cried.

So they asked the sheep to stand between them and the glory they feared.

Yet while their conductor remained upon the height, many below began to grow blind again.

They wandered from the path almost as soon as they had received it. When their leader descended and saw what had happened, he was filled with grief and severity. Some among the flock were struck down, and those who remained were brought again toward the fold.

I wondered at this. They had escaped wolves, crossed impossible waters, and seen their Lord deliver them, yet still their eyes could close.

Freedom from an oppressor does not always free the heart from blindness.

The sheep who had led them became as a man and built a house for the Lord among them. Generations passed. The companion who had once stood beside him died; the great sheep passed away; smaller sheep rose in their place. Their great conductor also died, and the flock mourned him bitterly before crossing another river into a land beautiful and abundant.

There they ate until they were satisfied.

Their house stood in the midst of a delightful country. Yet even there their sight remained uncertain. Sometimes their eyes opened; sometimes they became blind.

New leaders arose. One restored their sight and brought them back, but afterward dogs, foxes, and wild boars came against them. Then a ram rose from among the flock and fought fiercely, driving the beasts back on every side.

For a time he was mighty.

But I watched glory leave him.

The ram who had defended the flock began to strike the very sheep he had been raised to protect. Strength had entered his hands, and slowly he forgot why it had been given. The defender began to resemble the creatures he had fought.

This frightened me almost more than the wolves.

For wolves are easily recognized. Their teeth announce what they are. More dangerous is the moment when a sheep discovers that it too can wound.

I had watched an entire people carried from oppression into freedom, from wilderness into abundance, from blindness into sight—and still the struggle between light and darkness continued within them. No river crossed once can settle forever what lives inside the heart.

Remember this when power comes into your hands.

You may have been wounded and still learn to wound. You may have fought oppression and still become oppressive. You may have seen the waters divide and still become blind.

Therefore keep your eyes open.

The Lord who walks between the sheep and the wolves does not deliver us so that we may become better wolves. He leads us through the waters so that we may learn another way of living.

And perhaps this is among the hardest freedoms of all:

to suffer beneath teeth and yet refuse, when our hour of strength arrives, to grow teeth of our own.
