## 38 - The Shepherds Who Forgot the Flock

The ram who had once defended the flock had laid aside his glory, and the Lord of the sheep chose another in his place. The former faithful sheep went to this new one privately and spoke with him, and beneath the word of the Lord he rose as a ram, a prince among the flock. For a little while the two stood near one another, the first honoring the one who would follow him, while dogs continued to trouble the sheep from every side.

Then the new ram withdrew, and I saw the first fall beneath the dogs. The younger one remained and gathered the smaller sheep around him. He lived among them, fathered many, and at last he too died, as every leader among mortal creatures must.

After him arose a smaller ram.

He grew into a prince among the sheep, and under him the flock increased greatly. Dogs, foxes, and wild boars fled before him. He struck the beasts that had preyed upon his people until none could enter freely among the sheep and carry them away.

The house of the Lord grew large and wide in those days.

Upon it the sheep built a tower, higher than the house itself, rising above the surrounding land. I saw the Lord of the sheep stand upon that tower, and before him appeared a table filled in abundance. It seemed for a moment that the long wandering had reached its purpose: a people gathered, a house established, enemies driven back, and the Lord dwelling among his flock.

Yet prosperity did not cure their blindness.

The sheep began wandering again.

Some left the house and scattered in different directions, following paths of their own choosing. Their eyes dimmed as they went, and the Lord called certain sheep and sent them after the wanderers. These messengers cried out to the flock, warning them and calling them home.

But the sheep turned upon those who had been sent to them.

Some they killed.

I watched one escape from their hands. He leapt away and cried against those who desired his death, and the Lord rescued him and brought him upward to himself. Still more messengers were sent, and their voices moved through the flock like lamentations.

Again and again they called.

Again and again the sheep wandered.

Then something changed.

The Lord allowed the consequences of their wandering to overtake them. Lions entered their pastures. Tigers, wolves, foxes, and wild beasts came among them, tearing and devouring, until the land that had once seemed secure became a place of terror.

The house of their fathers was abandoned.

I cried out.

I had seen many terrible things by then, yet I could not watch the sheep being torn apart without pleading for them. “Lord of the sheep!” I cried with all the strength remaining in me. “Look how the beasts devour them!”

But he was silent.

That silence was harder for me to bear than thunder.

I cannot tell you that I understood it. There are moments in what I have seen when the purposes of judgment lie beyond the reach of my heart. I knew the sheep had wandered; I knew they had rejected those sent to call them home. Yet knowledge did not prevent me from grieving when teeth closed upon them.

Then the Lord appointed seventy shepherds and placed the flock beneath their care.

He numbered the sheep entrusted to them and gave the shepherds limits. Some destruction was permitted within the judgment that had come upon the flock, but the shepherds were not given freedom to destroy according to their own desire.

And another watcher was appointed.

“Observe the shepherds,” the Lord commanded him. “Record what they do.”

For the Lord knew what power does in hands that cease to remember they are servants.

Every sheep lost would be counted. Every act beyond what had been permitted would be recorded. The shepherds themselves would not know the full accounting taking place above them, but nothing they did would disappear from the memory of heaven.

Then I watched the terrible thing the Lord had foreseen.

The shepherds exceeded their command.

They killed more than had been entrusted to destruction. They left sheep exposed to lions and tigers. Wild boars entered the flock and fed upon them, while those appointed to guard the sheep became participants in their ruin.

And still the watcher counted.

One sheep.

Another.

Another.

No life vanished merely because a ruler considered it insignificant.

This I want you to understand, Mathusala: authority does not make a creature the owner of those placed beneath it. A shepherd remains a servant. A prince remains accountable. Even one who has received a command from heaven cannot enlarge that command to satisfy his own cruelty.

There are always those who imagine that judgment gives them permission to hate.

It does not.

I had seen the Lord himself judge, yet I had also seen him hear the cries of oppressed sheep. I had seen him break the power of wolves and lead trembling creatures through divided waters. Whatever mysteries lie within divine judgment, human cruelty cannot hide itself there.

At last the beasts reached the holy place.

The tower was burned.

The house was thrown down.

When I saw the flames climb the place where the Lord had once stood above the full table, grief pierced me. I remembered its days of beauty, the flock gathered around it, the tower rising toward heaven.

Now smoke rose from its ruins.

And I wept.

Yet somewhere beyond what I could see, the account was still being written.

The shepherds might forget.

The beasts might not care.

The sheep themselves might believe they had been abandoned and erased.

But heaven was counting.

And sometimes, when darkness has become so great that you can no longer understand the silence of God, this is the small light you must guard:

Nothing done to the vulnerable is forgotten.

Not one sheep is uncounted.
