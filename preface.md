## Preface

Long before I knew there was a *Book of Enoch*, I was fascinated by Enoch himself.

His appearance in the Bible is remarkably brief. Genesis gives him only a handful of verses, tucked into a genealogy filled with names, births, years, and deaths. Then suddenly the rhythm breaks. We are told that Enoch “walked with God,” and then he was no more, because God took him. That is nearly all we are given.

Yet those few words captured my imagination.

The Bible is filled with mighty figures. There are kings who rule nations, prophets who confront empires, warriors who win battles, and apostles who carry their message across the world. Their lives are remembered because of the extraordinary things they did. But Enoch seemed different to me.

Enoch was a friend of God.

At least, that was how I came to imagine him. Scripture does not tell us of kingdoms he conquered or monuments he built. It does not preserve long speeches attributed to him or recount great miracles performed by his hands. It simply remembers the character of his life: he walked with God.

There was something deeply comforting to me in that image. God may have His mighty prophets and kings, but perhaps He also has His simple friends—people known not primarily for their accomplishments, but for the direction in which their hearts are turned. Perhaps a life does not have to appear extraordinary from the outside to contain an extraordinary friendship with God.

Much later, I discovered that ancient readers had been captivated by Enoch too.

Sometime during the centuries before and around the beginnings of Christianity, a collection of Jewish apocalyptic writings grew around this mysterious figure. Today we know that collection as *1 Enoch*, or the *Book of Enoch*. Its authors imagined what the man who walked with God might have seen if the veil between earth and heaven had been opened before him.

The result is one of the strangest and most imaginative works to survive from the ancient Jewish world.

Enoch journeys beyond the familiar boundaries of the earth. He sees mountains of astonishing beauty, chambers of winds and stars, rivers of fire, imprisoned angels, heavenly tablets, places prepared for the dead, and the hidden workings of creation. He encounters the Watchers who abandoned their appointed place and brought violence upon the earth. He witnesses judgment, destruction, restoration, and mysterious visions of a coming righteous one.

For centuries, much of the Christian world knew this book primarily through echoes and fragments. The New Testament letter of Jude even preserves a prophecy attributed to Enoch, showing that traditions associated with him circulated among some of the earliest Christians. The complete work, however, survived principally through the Ethiopian Christian tradition, where *1 Enoch* remained part of the biblical canon of the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church. Fragments discovered among the Dead Sea Scrolls later demonstrated just how ancient many of these traditions were.

But this book is not intended primarily as a historical study of *1 Enoch*. There are excellent scholarly works for readers who want to explore its composition, textual history, relationship to Second Temple Judaism, and influence upon early Christianity.

What I wanted was something different.

I wanted to walk with Enoch.

I wanted to experience these visions not simply as ancient material to be analyzed, but as a story to be entered. I wanted to stand beside Enoch beneath impossible mountains, look upward at wandering stars, feel the heat of heavenly fire, hear the voices of angels, and share something of his bewilderment when the architecture of creation opened before him.

And I wanted to hear it in modern English, broken into readings that could be taken slowly—one vision, one journey, one mystery at a time.

That desire became this book.

What follows is not a new translation of *1 Enoch*. It is a contemplative literary retelling. I have sought to preserve the people, events, images, and theological movements of the ancient work while allowing its world to breathe through narrative description. At times I have lingered over an image that the ancient text passes in a sentence. At other times I have allowed Enoch to reflect upon what he has witnessed, asking what these strange journeys might teach a person attempting to walk faithfully through a troubled world.

And *1 Enoch* is unquestionably a book born from a troubled world.

Again and again it confronts violence, corruption, oppressive rulers, distorted knowledge, injustice, and the frightening possibility that wickedness has become too deeply rooted to overcome. Its imagery can be severe. Its judgments can be unsettling. Heaven itself sometimes feels enormous and terrifying.

Yet beneath all that thunder, I began to hear a quieter question.

**How does a person of light walk through a world in which darkness is real without becoming dark themselves?**

That question became the thread I followed through these readings.

Enoch's world is filled with rebellious angels, violent giants, corrupt shepherds, wicked kings, and generations that lose their way. Yet righteousness persists. The humble are remembered. The cries of the oppressed reach heaven. Swords are eventually laid down. Houses destroyed by corruption are replaced. What has been scattered is gathered, and again and again light appears where darkness seemed to have claimed everything.

This does not make *1 Enoch* an easy book. Nor have I tried to make it one. I have allowed its angels to remain strange, its judgments troubling, its cosmology enormous, and many of its mysteries unresolved.

But perhaps mystery is part of Enoch's gift.

The man who first fascinated me was not remembered because he mastered the mysteries of heaven. He was remembered because he walked with God. After all the journeys through celestial realms, all the visions of judgment, and all the secrets whispered by angels, I find myself returning to those simple words.

Perhaps that is enough for us as well.

We may live in times when the world feels darker than we know how to repair. We may never become kings, prophets, heroes, or people whose names survive for generations. Most of our acts of goodness may be small enough to disappear from human memory.

But we can still walk gently.

We can still refuse hatred.

We can still choose truth over falsehood, mercy over vengeance, and integrity over the temptation to become like the darkness we oppose.

We can still walk with God.

My hope is that these readings awaken your imagination, that you occasionally feel the wonder and terror of standing beside Enoch as heaven opens, and that somewhere among the stars, angels, mountains, fires, judgments, and visions of restoration, you discover something unexpectedly simple.

In a dark world, a human life can still become a bearer of light.

And perhaps God still delights in walking with His friends.
